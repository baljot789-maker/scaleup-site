Real results

Four builds, and what actually changed

Four builds, described by what broke and what changed. Two carry measured before-and-after numbers. Two are estimated from what the work took.

Whose work this is.

Systems I built in previous roles, anonymized to keep employers and colleagues out of it. The numbers below are what changed in each case.

01

A four-person team took on 15 more clients without a single new hire

A content agency of four was hand-writing and hand-optimizing every client blog and release. Capacity was capped by what four people could physically produce, so growth meant hiring, and hiring meant margin.

I built a per-client voice profile system and a drafting workflow with keyword and pillar-page mapping wired in, then handed it to three writers. About nine in ten drafts came back in the client's voice without heavy editing. The remaining one in ten still needed a human, which is why a human stayed in the loop.

Over roughly 18 months the agency went from 10 clients to 25 on the same headcount. The system didn't win the clients. It's the reason winning them didn't cost three salaries.

10 clients carried, up from 10, on the same four people
10 clients25 clients

Settles: "Growing means hiring, and I can't get headcount approved."

02

A launch week that ate 25 hours now takes 10

An in-house communications function ran entirely on one person's memory. No documented style, no findable boilerplate, no repeatable process for pitching. Every launch was rebuilt from scratch, and quality depended on who happened to be doing it.

I documented the actual working style first, then built the drafting workflow around it, plus pitch generation, per-outlet personalization, and one standard reporting format so results stopped being re-derived every cycle.

A launch week went from about 25 hours to about 10. Same output, same standards, measured against my own before-and-after as the person who owned the function. The larger result is that it stopped being mine. Anyone on the team could run a launch after that.

25 hours per launch week, down from 25
0 hrs25 hrs

Settles: "If the person who knows how to do this leaves, we're finished."

03

Ten people stopped re-solving the same problem every week

A marketing team of about ten was writing to a style guide nobody could locate. Every piece got an informal cleanup pass from whoever cared most, which meant the same corrections happened over and over in a dozen separate places.

I built one shared style and brand-terms layer that every downstream workflow reads from, so the rules live in a single place instead of being duplicated inside each person's process. Update it once, and everything inherits the change.

About 5 hours per person per week across 10 people, which is roughly 50 hours a week or 2,400 a year. That figure is estimated from what the work took rather than clocked.

Settles: "Everyone's fixing the same things separately and nobody notices the cost."

04

Eight people stopped writing every creative brief from scratch

Briefs were being rebuilt from a blank page every time, so two people briefing the same kind of work produced two different documents. The gaps only showed up later, when the deliverable came back wrong and nobody could point to what had actually been asked for.

I built a brief generator that reads the company's own corpus and brand guidelines, then produces the brief and the filled templates that go with it. Every brief clears the same standardised process, so the method is the same whoever starts it. Anything that gets complicated escalates on its own to a short meeting with the people who own the final deliverable, rather than being guessed at.

The change is that briefing stopped being a writing task and became a review task. The argument now happens over a draft that already exists, which is a much faster argument to have.

Settles: "Every brief looks different because everyone writes it differently."
