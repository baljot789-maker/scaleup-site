# The five years before retirement decide the thirty after

Most people start planning retirement the year they retire. The real work happens earlier, in a window most people let slip past: the five years on either side of the date.

Here's why that window matters, and what to do with it.

## Your balance is not a plan

A common first meeting goes like this. Someone arrives with a healthy account balance and a rule of thumb they read years ago. The balance has grown, so they assume they're on track.

One client, the owner of a dental practice in the Chicago suburbs (details changed for privacy), came in convinced she was set because her account had doubled in six years. Nearly everything doubled in those six years. That's a market, not a plan. When we tied her savings to her actual spending, the picture changed, and she had time to change with it.

The balance tells you what you have. It says nothing about whether it's enough, because "enough" depends on what your life costs.

## Why the five-year window is different

The danger has a name: sequence risk.

If the market drops 30 percent when you're 40, you have decades to recover, and you're still buying shares at lower prices. If the same drop lands the year after you retire, you're selling shares at the bottom to pay for groceries. Every share sold in a downturn is a share that never recovers with the market.

Same drop. Completely different outcome. The only variable is when it hits, and the years closest to your retirement date are the ones where a bad stretch does permanent damage.

We can't tell you what markets will do next year. No one can, and you should be skeptical of anyone who tries. What a plan can do is make sure no single bad year gets to decide how you live.

## De-risking is a taper, not a switch

People get the transition wrong in two opposite ways. Some flip everything to bonds the day they retire. Others never adjust at all and carry a working-years portfolio into a spending-years life.

Both miss the point. The shift is gradual, and the right pace depends on your timeline, your spending, and what other income you'll have. There is no universal split that fits everyone. That's a conversation with an advisor who knows your numbers, not a rule you apply from a blog post. This one included.

## Give your money a job description

The tool that makes every other decision easier is a spending plan with three buckets:

**Must pay.** Mortgage, insurance, food, healthcare. The bills that arrive whether the market cooperates or not.

**Want to live.** Travel, the lake house weekends, helping your kids. The reason you saved in the first place.

**Could cut.** The spending you'd trim in a rough year without much pain.

Once those three are on paper, your portfolio finally has a job: cover the first bucket reliably, fund the second sustainably, and keep the third as your shock absorber.

Most market anxiety, in our experience, isn't really about the market. It's about money that doesn't have a job description yet.

## Start with the window you have

If you're inside five years of retiring, or you think you might be, the checklist is short: know what your life costs, know what your savings can safely produce, and make sure a bad first year can't rewrite the whole story.
