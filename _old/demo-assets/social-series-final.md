## Post 1

Most retirement plans start five years too late.

Not because people are lazy. Because the account balance keeps going up, and a rising number feels like a plan.

It isn't. A balance tells you what you have. A plan tells you whether it's enough, and "enough" depends on what your life actually costs.

Two questions worth answering before your next statement arrives:

1. What does a normal month cost you today?
2. What would you want a normal month to look like when the paychecks stop?

If you can't answer both in a sentence, that's the work. Everything else in retirement planning is downstream of those two numbers.

## Post 2

A 30 percent market drop at age 40 and a 30 percent drop at age 66 are not the same event.

At 40, you have decades to recover. You're still buying, so lower prices quietly work in your favor.

At 66, freshly retired, you're selling shares at the bottom to pay real bills. Every share sold in a downturn is gone. It never rides the recovery back up.

Advisors call this sequence risk. Plain version: what matters is when markets move relative to your retirement date, at least as much as how far they move.

The five years on either side of that date are where the risk concentrates. Which means they're also where planning pays off most.

---

## Post 3

"Should I move to bonds before I retire?"

Wrong shape of question. De-risking isn't a switch you flip on your last day of work. It's a taper.

Flip everything to bonds at once and you may run out of growth in a retirement that lasts 30 years. Never adjust and one bad year early on can do permanent damage.

The right pace depends on your spending, your timeline, and what other income you'll have. There's no universal number, and you should be wary of anyone who hands you one without asking about your life first.

## Post 4

A client came to us convinced she was ready to retire. Practice owner, diligent saver, account had doubled in six years. (Details changed for privacy.)

Here's what made the conversation hard: almost everything had doubled in those six years. Her balance wasn't proof of a plan. It was proof of a market.

When we tied her savings to what her life actually costs, she was short. Not hopelessly, because she found out with five years of runway left. Enough time to adjust her saving, her timeline, and her portfolio's job.

That's the whole argument for planning early. Not fear. Time to act while acting is cheap.
