[04:10] INTERVIEWER: So when someone walks in five years out from retiring, what's the first thing you look at?

[04:16] PRIYA: Honestly? The first thing I look at is whether they've looked at anything. Most people haven't. They have a 401k balance and a guess. And the guess is usually based on a rule of thumb they read somewhere in 2011.

[04:31] INTERVIEWER: Can you give me an example of what that looks like in practice?

[04:35] PRIYA: Sure, so, take Susan Whitfield. She's a dentist up in Naperville, owns her practice, and she came to us last spring convinced she was fine because her account had doubled since 2018. And I had to sit her down and go, Susan, everything doubled since 2018, that's not a plan, that's a market. Her actual spending number said she was two years short.

[05:02] INTERVIEWER: What did you do for her?

[05:04] PRIYA: We restructured. Moved her onto our balanced model, which, I mean, it did fourteen percent last year, we beat the S&P doing it with less risk, so that conversation got a lot easier. But the point isn't the return. The point is she finally had a number tied to her actual life.

[05:24] INTERVIEWER: You mentioned the five-year window a lot. Why five years?

[05:28] PRIYA: Because that's when sequence risk starts to bite. If the market drops thirty percent when you're forty, fine, you have time, it's basically a sale. If it drops thirty percent the year after you retire, you're selling shares at the bottom to buy groceries, and the math never forgives you for that. The five years on either side of your retirement date are the ones that decide everything.

[05:53] INTERVIEWER: So what should people actually do in that window?

[05:57] PRIYA: Look, I'll just say it plainly. If you're within five years of retiring, you should have at least thirty percent in bonds. And if you're over fifty and not maxing the catch-up contribution, you're leaving free tax money on the table. Full stop. Those two things, everyone, every time.

[06:18] INTERVIEWER: Is there anything people get wrong about that advice?

[06:21] PRIYA: They treat it like a light switch. They flip everything to bonds the day they retire, or they never de-risk at all. Both are wrong. It's a runway, not a cliff. You taper. And the taper should match your actual spending plan, which means you need a spending plan, which brings us back to where we started. Most people don't have one.

[06:47] INTERVIEWER: What does a real spending plan look like?

[06:50] PRIYA: Three buckets, roughly. What has to get paid no matter what, the mortgage, insurance, food. What you want your life to look like, travel, the lake house weekends, helping the kids. And then the stuff you'd cut if you had to. Once those are on paper, the portfolio conversation gets easy, because now the portfolio has a job. Before that it's just a number that goes up and down and makes you feel things.

[07:21] INTERVIEWER: That's a good line.

[07:23] PRIYA: It's true though. The portfolio has to have a job. When people are anxious about the market, nine times out of ten it's because their money doesn't have a job description yet.
